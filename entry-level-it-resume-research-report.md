# Bad Resumes vs. Good Resumes for Entry-Level IT: A Research Report — With a Direct Fix for Uriah Raffer's Resume

## TL;DR
- **Your instinct is correct: your resume is too dense, and it is the single highest-leverage thing to fix.** The evidence is unambiguous — Ladders' 2018 eye-tracking study found "the average initial screening time for a candidate's resume clocks in at just 7.4 seconds—an improvement on the six-second average screening time found in 2012," the biggest failure mode in that window is a "wall of text," and your Projects section (one bullet runs ~65 words), your four-line professional summary, and your ~40-item Technical Skills block are exactly the parts that die in a skim.

Target 40–50% white space and a genuine one page.
- **Maintain two versions.** A lean, skim-optimized, ATS-safe one-pager for private-sector help desk/support/SOC roles; and a separate, more complete package for California state jobs (CalCareers) — where the STD 678 must contain full duty descriptions, an SOQ is a scored writing sample, and the *current* Information Technology Associate exam is a 100%-weighted multiple-choice test of 50 items (not the older Training & Experience self-report), so "completeness" lives in the application form and SOQ, not in a bloated resume.

## Key Findings

1. **The 6–7 second skim is real, and density is its primary casualty.** Ladders' eye-tracking research found recruiters spent ~6 seconds per resume in 2012 and 7.4 seconds in 2018 on the initial screen. Dense text blocks get skipped; recruiters fixate on name, current title/employer, dates, and education. Ladders' own guidance: "short declarative statements are easier to process—and therefore likely to be more memorable—than paragraph-length descriptions."

2. **The "ATS auto-rejects you" fear is largely a myth — the real gatekeeper is a time-pressed human.** Enhancv's Sept–Oct 2025 study of 25 U.S. recruiters found that 23 of 25 (92%) said their systems do not auto-reject resumes for formatting, content, or design; only 2 of 25 (8%) configure content-based auto-rejection. The famous "75% of resumes are auto-rejected" stat traces to Preptel, a résumé-optimization vendor whose product ("ResumeterPro") pitched the figure around 2012 and which shut down in August 2013 without ever publishing a methodology. What ATS actually does is *parse and rank* — so formatting that breaks parsing (tables, columns, text boxes, header/footer contact info, graphics, icons) is the real risk, not a keyword-scoring robot.

3. **For entry-level IT, one page is still the right target — but for content reasons, not an arbitrary rule.** The strongest evidence (ResumeGo's Oct–Nov 2018 study of 482 hiring professionals and 7,712 resumes) shows recruiters were "2.3 times as likely to prefer two-page resumes" overall — but only "1.4 times as likely" at entry level, and several recruiters read a tight one-pager from a junior candidate as evidence of the ability to prioritize. With <1 year of professional IT experience, you should be on one page.

4. **The specific failure modes that sink entry-level IT resumes** are density/wall-of-text, overlong multi-line bullets, dense summaries that duplicate the resume, buzzword/keyword stuffing, skills laundry-lists with no supporting evidence, cert-dumping without context, outdated objective statements, generic non-tailored resumes, ATS-hostile formatting, duty-based bullets with no outcomes, skill inflation, and poorly presented homelabs (either omitted or listed like a shopping list of tools).

5. **Homelabs and personal projects are genuinely valued for entry-level IT** — but only when framed as accomplishments with outcomes, not tool inventories. Treat them like jobs: title, action verb, what you built, what it demonstrates.

6. **State and private sector reward opposite things.** Private sector: lean, skim-optimized, keyword-aligned, one page. California state: the STD 678 form must carry complete duty descriptions ("See Resume" is explicitly discouraged), the SOQ is a scored narrative writing sample, and completeness/detail is rewarded in those instruments — not by cramming the resume.

## Details

### 1. Bad Resumes — the catalog of failure modes and *why* each one fails

**Wall-of-text density / cramped formatting.** This is the master failure and the one most relevant to you. The recruiter's initial pass is a 6–7.4-second skim (Ladders), and dense blocks are precisely what the eye skips. Practitioner guidance quantifies it: below ~30% white space a resume reads as a "wall of text" that recruiters skip; 40–50% is the comfortable scanning zone. A useful diagnostic is the "squint test" — hold the page at arm's length and squint; if you see a single dark rectangle, it's too dense. *Why it fails:* dense text raises the cognitive cost of the skim, so the recruiter finds nothing in the window they allot and moves on.

**Tiny fonts and margin abuse.** The standard is 10–12pt body text and 0.5–1" margins. Shrinking to 9pt or cutting margins below 0.5" to cram content is self-defeating: cramped margins can literally clip the first characters of each line in some renderers, and recruiters read tiny margins as a signal that "you had too much content and squeezed margins rather than editing." *Why it fails:* it trades the one thing that helps a skim (white space) for the one thing that hurts it (density).

**Overlong bullets and too many bullets per role.** The consensus formula is 1–2 lines per bullet (~15–25 words) and 3–5 bullets for a recent/relevant role, fewer for older ones. Bullets that run 3+ lines should be split or cut. *Why it fails:* a bullet longer than two lines stops being scannable and becomes a paragraph; and 7–8 bullets dilute the 2–3 that actually matter.

**Dense professional summaries that repeat the resume.** A summary should be 2–3 lines. A six-or-seven-sentence summary "looks more like a cover letter" and wastes the most valuable real estate on the page. *Why it fails:* it burns the prime top-of-page zone (where the eye lands first) restating what's below instead of making an immediate, differentiated case.

**Buzzword/keyword stuffing.** Recruiters report fatigue with generic descriptors ("team player," "results-driven," "detail-oriented," "fast learner"). One recruiter's line: "If you tell me you're 'detail-oriented,' I assume you have nothing specific to say about your work." Modern ATS increasingly use semantic matching, so raw keyword stuffing can be flagged rather than rewarded. *Why it fails:* buzzwords consume space without conveying evidence, and they signal a lack of concrete achievement.

**Skills laundry-lists with no supporting evidence.** A skills section of 20+ disconnected terms performs worse: an analysis cited by resume practitioners found resumes with 20+ separately listed skills had a much higher rejection rate than the same skills woven into experience. As one writer put it, "'I see you have the skill of Microsoft Office — you're hired,' said no recruiter ever." *Why it fails:* an unsupported keyword proves nothing; the same term inside an achievement bullet proves capability.

**Cert-dumping without context.** Listing certifications is good; but a stack of acronyms with no dates and no connection to the target role reads as noise. Best practice: name the cert, the issuer, and the date. *Why it fails:* context (recency, relevance) is what converts an acronym into a credential.

**Objective statements and other outdated conventions.** The modern consensus is that objective statements are outdated for most candidates because they describe what *you* want rather than what you deliver; a professional summary is the standard. Objectives survive only in narrow cases (true career pivots). *Why it fails:* an objective spends prime space on the candidate's wishes, which recruiters don't prioritize.

**Generic, non-tailored resumes.** The same resume for every posting is the single biggest strategic error after density. Recruiters and modern parsers both recognize their own language; if the posting says "Salesforce," "CRM software" won't match. *Why it fails:* it fails the keyword alignment that both the ranking software and the human use to judge fit.

**Poor ATS formatting.** Tables, multi-column layouts, text boxes, graphics/skill bars, icons, headshots, and putting contact info in the document header/footer all break parsing. ATS reads linearly (left→right, top→bottom); columns produce "word salad," and header/footer contact details are frequently dropped entirely. Use a single-column layout, standard section headings ("Experience," "Education," "Skills," not "My Journey" / "The Toolkit"), web-safe fonts (Arial, Calibri, Georgia, Times New Roman), simple round/dash bullets, consistent MM/YYYY dates, and a text-based PDF exported from Word/Docs (never a scanned image). *Why it fails:* if the parser scrambles or drops a section, the keywords never register regardless of content quality.

**Vague duty-based bullets with no outcomes.** "Responsible for providing technical support" describes a job description, not a person. The fix is the Action Verb + Task/Technology + Result (APR / X-Y-Z) formula. A survey cited by resume writers found a large share of hiring managers pass over resumes with no measurable results. *Why it fails:* duties tell the reader what the role was; achievements tell them whether you were good at it.

**Exaggeration and skill inflation.** Listing a tool you touched once as a core competency invites an interview question you can't answer. Recruiters warn that inflated self-report is the classic weakness of experience-based screening. *Why it fails:* it converts a screen-in into a screen-out the moment it's probed.

**Poorly presented homelab/personal projects.** Two opposite errors: omitting/underselling them ("just hobbies"), or listing them as a shopping list of tools with no outcome. *Why it fails:* for a candidate whose strength is projects rather than tenure, a tool-dump wastes the most persuasive material you have.

**Burying relevant experience under irrelevant jobs; bad file formats/filenames; typos and inconsistency.** Irrelevant roles should be compressed to make room for relevant ones; files should be named professionally (e.g., `Uriah-Raffer-Resume.pdf`) and submitted as text PDFs; and typos/inconsistent formatting (mixed date formats, mixed tenses) read as carelessness — especially damaging in IT, where the resume itself is a proxy for attention to detail.

### 2. Good Resumes — the patterns that work for entry-level IT

**Structure/section order for a degree + certs + internship + homelab candidate.** The recommended order when your strength is credentials and projects rather than tenure:
1. **Header** (name; city/state; phone; professional email; LinkedIn; GitHub) — in the body, never a header/footer.
2. **Professional Summary** — 2–3 lines, value-first, tailored.
3. **Certifications** (or a combined "Education & Certifications") — near the top for entry-level, because certs are among the first things IT screeners look for.
4. **Experience** — internship first.
5. **Projects / Homelab** — treated as legitimate technical experience.
6. **Skills** — clustered, job-relevant.
(Education can sit near the top for a recent grad; certs and a relevant internship generally earn the highest position.)

**Ideal length.** One page for entry-level. The two-page research (ResumeGo) shows the *general* preference for two pages is weakest at entry level (1.4x vs. 2.3x overall), and multiple recruiters read a tight junior one-pager as evidence of prioritization skill. Don't pad to two pages.

**White space and visual hierarchy.** Target ~40–50% white space; 10–12pt body, 14–16pt headings; 1.0–1.15 line spacing; consistent spacing between sections; bold for job titles and section headers (bold parses fine and aids the skim).

**Bullet construction formula.** Action verb + tool/technology + quantified outcome. Weak: "Responsible for customer service." Strong: "Resolved 90+ daily client inquiries via [ticketing system], maintaining 98% satisfaction." Keep to 1–2 lines; 3–5 bullets for recent/relevant roles.

**Presenting certifications, a WGU degree, an internship, and homelab credibly.**
- *Certs:* name + issuer + date; group the CompTIA stack cleanly.
- *WGU degree:* competency-based and legitimate — list "B.S. Network Engineering and Security, Western Governors University, Conferred October 2025." No need to explain the model.
- *Internship:* lead the Experience section with it; it's your strongest, most relevant signal.
- *Homelab/projects:* title them like roles ("Home Lab Administrator — Personal Project") and use outcome-oriented bullets, not tool lists.

**Framing customer service (tax prep) as transferable.** Reframe Block Advisors around IT-relevant transferable skills: communicating complex information to non-technical people, working under deadline/SLA-like pressure, handling sensitive data, and high-volume client throughput. This directly supports the "50% of IT is explaining technical things to non-technical people" reality that hiring managers screen for.

**Tailoring and keyword alignment done right.** Mirror the exact language of each posting (both acronym and full term — "SIEM (Microsoft Sentinel)") in a skills section and naturally within bullets; don't paste the whole job ad. Build one master resume and tailor in ~10 minutes per application.

**ATS-safe but human-readable.** Single column, standard headings, web-safe font, no tables/columns/graphics, contact info in the body, text PDF. This satisfies both the parser and the 7-second human skim simultaneously.

**Annotated strong example (junior IT support / SOC), abbreviated:**
> **Experience — IT Support Intern, [Company] (2025–Present)**
> • Resolved 40+ tier-1 tickets/week across Windows 10/11, hardware, and account issues, maintaining a 95% first-contact resolution rate.
> • Administered Active Directory accounts (provisioning, group membership, password resets) for [N] users.
>
> Each bullet leads with an action verb, names the technology, and quantifies — scannable in the 7-second window.

### 3. How resumes are actually read

**The skim.** Initial screen ≈ 6–7.4 seconds (Ladders 2012/2018). Recruiters fixate on ~6 points: name, current title, current employer, dates, previous title, education, reading in a top-left-heavy F-pattern (a well-documented Nielsen Norman Group reading behavior). Serious candidates then get a longer 2–4-minute read.

**ATS reality vs. myth.** ATS is near-universal (used by the overwhelming majority of large employers), but it primarily *parses and ranks*, it does not usually auto-reject. Enhancv's 2025 recruiter interviews: 23 of 25 (92%) say their systems don't auto-reject on content/formatting/design; only 2 of 25 (8%) enable content auto-rejection. The only routine automatic rejections are *knockout questions* (work authorization, onsite availability, minimum-years thresholds) — not keyword scores. Modern systems (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby) parse cleanly and don't content-filter; Workday and Taleo are stricter parsers, which is where a clean single-column layout matters most. Practical implication: optimize for the human skim first, keep formatting parser-safe, and mirror the posting's real keywords honestly.

**Entry-level IT screening in practice.** MSPs and staffing agencies move fast on high-volume help-desk reqs, screening for certs, specific tools, and communication ability, often with a recruiter phone screen plus a client technical interview. TEKsystems (an Allegis Group company) is the largest US IT staffing firm — it topped Staffing Industry Analysts' 2026 "Largest IT Staffing Firms in the United States" list with $3.92 billion in 2025 IT staffing revenue, ahead of Insight Global ($3.28B); Robert Half Technology is another major player for help-desk placements. Enterprises use ATS ranking plus recruiter triage. The common denominator: a fast human scan for role-relevant proof.

### 4. Density — measured, diagnosed, and fixed

**What "too dense" means, in measurable terms:**
- **White space:** aim 40–50%; below ~30% reads as a wall of text.
- **Bullets:** 1–2 lines each (~15–25 words); split anything 3+ lines.
- **Bullets per role:** 3–5 for recent/relevant; 2–3 for older.
- **Summary:** 2–3 lines, never a 5–7-sentence paragraph.
- **Font/spacing:** 10–12pt body, 1.0–1.15 line spacing, ≥0.5" margins.
- **Sentence length:** long single sentences (30+ words) are a density red flag.

**The readability cost of density.** In a 7-second skim, a dense page yields *zero* extracted signal — the recruiter finds nothing and moves on. Density doesn't just bury weak content; it buries your strongest content (your quantified wins) alongside it.

**How to diagnose it.** Squint test (dark rectangle = too dense); count lines per bullet; count words per bullet; measure the ratio of ink to white space; check whether any single bullet exceeds two lines.

**Concrete techniques to fix a dense resume:**
1. **Cut the weakest bullets.** Keep the top 2–3 achievements per role.
2. **Tighten phrasing.** Delete filler ("a," "the," "in order to"); remove hedges ("roughly," "about") where a clean number reads better.
3. **Trim/split the summary** to 2–3 value-first lines.
4. **Convert prose to scannable structure** — turn multi-clause narrative bullets into single action-verb-plus-outcome lines.
5. **Cluster and cut the skills list** to job-relevant groupings.
6. **Prioritize ruthlessly** — the goal isn't to include everything you've done; it's to make your best 3–4 proofs impossible to miss.

### 5. State vs. Private Sector

**Private sector (help desk, desktop support, IT support specialist, service desk, junior sysadmin, tier-1 SOC):** lean, skim-optimized, one page, keyword-aligned, ATS-safe. Everything in Sections 1–4 applies directly.

**California state (CalCareers / Information Technology Associate):** the conventions genuinely differ, and detail is rewarded — *but in the application instruments, not a bloated resume:*
- **The STD 678 (state application) must carry complete duty descriptions for each position.** California guidance explicitly discourages "See Resume" under duties (e.g., "Please DO NOT indicate 'See Resume' under duties performed"), and resumes are "not accepted in lieu of" the STD 678. Fill out the form fully.
- **The SOQ (Statement of Qualifications) is a scored selection instrument** — CalHR describes it as something that "may be used as part of the application screening process and can serve as a writing sample and/or written interview." It uses set prompts drawn from the duty statement; reviewers score responses (often "Not Qualified / Qualified / Well Qualified") against pre-set criteria, frequently using STAR-structured responses. Follow the exact format spec (page limit, font, font size) — deviations can disqualify. This is where completeness and detail pay off.
- **The Information Technology Associate exam itself:** as of the current bulletin (Exam Code 2PBBJ), this is a **100%-weighted multiple-choice written exam** — the CalHR ITA Study Guide states "The examination contains 50 multiple-choice items in one content section: Information Technology Associate," and "A final score of 70% must be attained to be placed on the eligible list." This *replaced* the older Training & Experience self-report evaluation used through the 1/3/2022 bulletin (7PB33), which had been "Training and Experience Evaluation – Weighted 100% of the final score." Passing places you on a rank-ordered eligibility list, where the top three ranks are typically "reachable." Because the exam is now a test rather than a self-reported experience inventory, your resume's job in the state process is narrower: support the STD 678 and SOQ, not carry the scoring.
- **Duty-statement mirroring:** tailor the STD 678 and SOQ (and a state-version resume) to the posting's duty-statement language.

**Should you maintain two versions? Yes.**
- *Private version:* the lean one-pager described above.
- *State version:* a resume that supports (never substitutes for) a fully completed STD 678 and a tailored SOQ; it can be slightly more complete, but the completeness properly belongs in the form and SOQ. Don't let the state process tempt you into re-densifying your private-sector resume.

## Caveats
- **The 6–7-second figure describes the *initial screen*, not total review.** Serious candidates get a 2–4-minute read; the skim determines whether you earn it. Some recruiters dispute the exact number, but the underlying behavior (fast triage, dense text skipped, top-left F-pattern) is well established.
- **ATS advice online is heavily commercialized.** Many "ATS score" tools and "beat the bot" services sell fear; the "75% auto-rejected" stat is unsubstantiated (it traces to the now-defunct vendor Preptel). The defensible takeaways: keep formatting parser-safe and mirror real keywords; don't obsess over invented match scores.
- **The California ITA exam type changed recently.** The current bulletin (2PBBJ) describes a 100%-weighted multiple-choice exam; the older (through-2022, 7PB33) bulletin described a Training & Experience evaluation. If you encounter an older bulletin or a different IT classification, confirm the exam type on that specific bulletin, because it changes how much your self-reported experience matters to scoring.
- **Two-page resume research is real but doesn't apply to you yet.** The 2.3x preference is an all-levels average and is weakest at entry level (1.4x); as you gain years, revisit length.
- **Staffing-firm revenue figures vary by source and methodology.** The $3.92B/$3.28B (SIA 2026) figures reflect IT-staffing-specific revenue; other rankings cite broader totals. Treat them as directional evidence of scale, not precise figures.
- Some cited resume-advice sources are vendor blogs; where possible, primary/institutional sources (Ladders, ResumeGo, university career centers, CalHR/CalCareers, recruiter interview studies) were prioritized.
